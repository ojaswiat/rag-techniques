3rd Assessment: Dissertation (70%)
For the third and final assessment you should submit your dissertataion. It should be self-contained, clear and concise, but cover everything you want to say, since this is your final opportunity to describe your project. Please make the dissertation to be at most 50 pages including all the title page, acknowledgement, declaration and references, but NOT appendix. I sincerely believe you don't need more than 50 pages to describe the essential details of your project and what you have learned. If you feel you need more than 50 pages, you can use an appendix to describe additional details. There is NO word count.

The reader should be able to understand what you have done, and why you have done it, without referring to any other documents or submissions for previous assessments. This will probably be one of the largest pieces of writing you've ever undertaken.

Writing coherently over the course of many sections is a skill that you might not be familiar with, so seek advice from your supervisor. Remember that your final module mark is based largely on what you write. A good dissertation can help to rescue a project that didn't go to plan, provided you can explain why with some critical self-reflection.

This guidance splits design and implementation into separate sections, which makes sense for some projects and helps you to understand what to include. However, you might feel more comfortable if you cover the design and implementation together in one bigger section (perhaps with sub-sections to help readability). There is nothing wrong with this approach. Modern software development takes place in a more agile way, with aspects of design and implementation happening at the same time. The important thing is to tell the story of how your software was developed, including how it was designed and implemented. It doesn't matter how you present these two parts, and there is no right or wrong way to go about it. Don't worry if your friends use a different structure, because your project is unique to you.

Your dissertation should be a formal piece of writing, so you should use proper academic language. Take care to proof-read your work and pay attention to spelling and grammar. English proficiency is one of the marking criteria. There are no set standards for page layout, margins, fonts, line spacing and so on.

Avoid Text Book Content
A common mistake in writing dissertations is a reliance on text book content to inflate your dissertation. You don't need to tell the reader things they should already know. It's fine to say that you followed the waterfall model, but you don't need to use lots of words to explain what that means. Similarly, you might have UML diagrams, ERDs and so on. You can assume the reader already knows what these are, so don't waste space explaining the syntax. Instead you should discuss the content of diagrams. In general, focus on the original aspects of your own work.

Dedication & Acknowledgements
These pages are the one place where you can write in the first person and speak with some emotion. Authors use these pages for different reasons, but typically they dedicate their work to family or friends, and acknowledge the contribution of proof-readers, supervisors, friends, or anyone who helped with the project.

Abstract
The abstract should be a clear, informative and concise summary of your project. Two or three paragraphs is the norm, and it should definitely fit on one page maximum. You will probably write this after the main bulk of the dissertation is finished, so create the page as a placeholder and then return to fill it in. You should describe what you did and how you did it, including any major recommendations or conclusions. The purpose of the abstract is to allow any future readers to decide whether your dissertation would be useful without having to read the entire thing.

Statement of Ethical Compliance
Your dissertation should have a statement of ethical compliance within the first few pages. The best place for it would be just after the abstract. This should state the data and participant categories for your project (for example, A0, C2, etc.) with a sentence to confirm that you followed our ethical guidance.

Table of Contents
The table of contents should list all the major sections in your dissertation to one level of nesting. Include the page number for each section. Modern word processors can automatically generate this table if you've used heading styles. Alternatively you can type the table manually when you've finished the rest of the document.

A table of figures is also useful if you have lots of diagrams and illustrations, but it isn't compulsory. Number your figures consecutively, even if they are different types. Screenshots, diagrams, illustrations, tables and code snippets are all 'figures' in this context. Paying attention to these small details really helps to make your work look more polished.

Reusing your earlier work
A great deal of the background material in your introduction will have already been covered in your first assessment. It's okay to copy text and reuse your own material in the dissertation, although you might want to expand on it and discuss things in more depth. You can find some extra words by discussing why things changed as the project progressed.

Introduction & background
The purpose of the introduction is to give the reader sufficient knowledge and understanding to tackle the rest of the dissertation. Put your work in context and refer to previous studies or similar software. Clearly state the problem you are trying to address and the motivation for making the software. Discuss background material and show how it is relevant, using correct citations. State your aims and requirements as a series of bullet points, or number them if you want to refer to them later in the dissertation. The main body of the dissertation should show how your software design and implementation met these requirements.

If your dissertation uses any specific terminology or acronyms that might be unfamiliar to a general computer science audience, explain them. Consider using a glossary of terms. You can assume that the reader will be able to program and understand code, although they might not be familiar with the language you used.

If you're not sure what to put in the introduction, leave it until towards the end. Write the other sections first and then note down all the assumptions you made about the reader's knowledge and background. Go back and provide this knowledge in the introduction, clearly covering anything that's obscure, technical or non-standard.

Requirements analysis
If you conducted a requirements analysis activity with human participants, describe how you chose them and what the activity involved. Include an example participant consent form in an appendix. The requirements should already have been listed in the introduction, so make sure nobody can be individually identified. You don't need to include this section if you did not conduct a requirements analysis with other people.

Design
The majority of projects will involve the creation of a piece of original software. This section should provide a detailed design for your software or system, using accepted notation and techniques. Every design will be unique, but consider including information about the following items.

The components of the system and how they are organised.
The data structures and algorithms used by the system.
The design of the user interface, including screen mockups, sketches and screenshots.
The flow of navigation from one part of the UI to another (particularly important for mobile apps and websites).
You will probably want to use UML diagrams or some other design notation. The exact format of the design is up to you. Consider use case diagrams, interaction diagrams, class diagrams, pseudocode, data dictionaries, data flow diagrams, screen layouts, and anything else that you feel is relevant. You should include paragraphs of text to help explain your design. Provide enough detail to show that your system is well-designed and built on solid foundations, but don't overwhelm the reader.

If your project has more of a research focus and applies existing software to a particular problem, this section should cover the design of your experimental process, including how you controlled for variables (in a non-coding sense), how your results were measured, and how you can be sure the research is robust and replicable. You might have developed your own software to carry out the research, in which case you'll also need to cover its design (as explained above).

Implementation (Realisation)
Each dissertation is unique, so there are no formal requirements for what to cover in the main part of the document. However, you will need to talk about the implementation of your software (or the realisation of the experimental plan). If your software is particularly complex or has multiple parts, you might split this across several sub-sections.

This section should cover how you tackled the development, any technical achievements you want to highlight, problems encountered, and so on. You should clearly describe what you have developed and how it all works. If you kept a project logbook you can use it to refresh your memory. Include the things that didn't work, and any dead ends you encountered, as well as things that went well. Don't simply present a sanitised version of the final product. Including the less successful aspects helps to demonstrate the reality of the development process.

Illustrations, tables and diagrams help to add visual appeal to your dissertation. Large chunks of text can be boring. Try to structure your document so there is at least one section heading, table, diagram, or other visual distraction (such as a code snippet) on each page. You won’t always be able to escape a page of plain text, so make sure it keeps the reader interested.

Testing & Evaluation
You should describe the steps you took to test your software components as they were developed. This might include a schedule of unit tests and their results. Your evaluation is a separate activity and involves looking at the software as a whole to see if it meets your original aims and requirements.

You'll probably ask other people to use your software and give you feedback, so you should describe how you chose them and what the activity involved. Include an example participant consent form in an appendix. If you used a questionnaire or interview, include the original questions in an appendix and present the results in aggregated form in this section. If you obtained text comments or feedback from participants, include an anonymised summary. If you updated or changed your software after getting feedback, explain what you did. In most cases you won't have time to implement changes, so explain what you would do if you had more time.

Project Ethics
You must include a section on project ethics somewhere in your dissertation, but you can put it wherever it makes most sense, which will usually be towards the end or near the evaluation section. Remember our ethical guidance and think about what you've already written in your detailed proposal concerning data sources, project ethics and human participants. Your dissertation should cover these topics in greater detail. You can reuse your previous text, expanding and amending it to reflect what actually took place during the project. Be mindful that the markers will be looking for evidence that you followed our ethical guidance. If your project genuinely has no ethical considerations, simply include a sentence to explain why the ethical guidance does not apply.

Conclusion & Future Work
The introduction and conclusion are two halves of the same thing. Together they establish what you have done, why you have done it, and whether or not you were successful. Think of them as a pair of brackets. Whatever you opened in the introduction you should close in the conclusion. Show how you met your aims and requirements, referring back to earlier sections of the dissertation.

You should try to leave the reader fulfilled and in a positive mood. Even if you didn't achieve everything you hoped, you can still discuss the reasons why in a positive manner. Very few projects are complete pieces of work. There will always be something extra you could have done, or something you might do differently with hindsight. Discussing these will help to show that you recognise the wider context of your project.

References
List references to articles, websites, videos, datasets, journals, and any other background reading you carried out while writing the dissertation and implementing your project. Be sure to cite every quotation, every idea that was expressed by someone else before you, and anything which is not in your own words. Remember to reference diagrams if you have copied them from another source.

Make sure you properly cite and reference any code that isn't yours, as well as any graphics or music (or anything else) that was made by someone else. Appendices
Appendices are used for bulky or unwieldy content that would break the flow of the main body. They shouldn't contain anything new, so everything should be mentioned or discussed in the main body. Some examples are listed below.

Instruction sheets or questionnaires given to people who helped test and evaluate your software.

Any tables and diagrams that need to be included but are too big to put in the main body.
Your dissertation should still make sense even if the appendices are missing. If this is not the case, you might like to move portions of the appendices into the main body. Getting the balance right is tricky, but the key thing is to use appendices to help keep the main body flowing smoothly.

Do not include all source code as an appendix. Only include code if you want to refer to it and discuss a particular aspect in the dissertation. Where possible, include code snippets in the main body, and only place it in an appendix if it's too big to go anywhere else.

General Considerations
All data presented in your project must be genuine. In other words, you must have generated it yourself or performed original analysis of data from a secondary source.
Any data analysis should be your own work. Academic misconduct, including plagiarism, is a serious offence that can result in severe penalties.
You should gain permission to access data and should not break any laws to obtain data. Note that websites can be subject to copyright laws, so you should think carefully before employing web scraping as part of your project.
Your project should be carried out in the UK, and involve only participants based in the UK.
Your project must not involve the use of covert research (ie. where participants are not aware you are doing research).
You must not offer anyone money, prizes or other financial inducements to take part in your project.
 

Public & Research Data Sources
You are allowed to use open data sourcesLinks to an external site. provided by governments and other organisations, open resourcesLinks to an external site. generated by publicly funded research, and data from public APIsLinks to an external site.. Check licensing terms, copyright notices and fair use policies to be certain you have permission to use the data before you access or download it. If you are in any doubt, check with your supervisor. Human Subjects, Human Tissue and Animals
Your project must not directly involve any experiments with human subjects, human tissue, or animals, as this falls well outside our ethical boundaries. Note that you can use data derived from experiments or studies that other people have published, provided it is fully anonymised and does not identify any individuals. For example, you could train a machine learning model with a database of urine samples, or build a visualisation from a database of bird migration patterns. Data must be fully anonymised before you access or download it, and you must have permission to process it.

 

Data Categories
Your project will fall into one of the following data categories, depending on what type of data you are using. Note that ethical considerations only apply to data about humans and animals. If you're using another kind of data (or no data at all), you would select category A.

A	No use of data derived from humans or animals
B	Anonymous data about humans or animals from open sources or public APIs
C	Anonymous data about humans extracted from social media via public APIs
D	Anonymous medical data about humans from open sources
E	Published data sets involving identifiable humans (such as face pictures) from open sources
Social media companies such as Twitter and Reddit have public APIs through which you can access data, and these fall within category C. Provided you access them with permission, you can use these APIs to extract anonymous data, such as the text of tweets. However, you must not store usernames or real names that could be used to identify individuals. If these are provided as part of the API data, you should discard them or use a one-way hash function to obfuscate them. You should not access images from social media if they are likely to contain identifiable humans. Other images can be accessed according to the terms and conditions for the relevant API.

Category D includes medical data derived from humans, provided it has already been fully anonymised before you access it. This also includes images of small parts of the human body from which no individual could be identified. You might be building an app to detect diabetes by looking at retinal scans, or an app to detect melanoma from photos of skin blemishes. These types of project are permitted provided the database of images comes from an open source, and you have permission to access it.

Data from category E can only be used in very limited circumstances. For example, if you are building an app to scan faces and detect emotion, you will need a large database of face pictures to train your machine learning model. Such databases do exist for academic and research purposes, and should have already been identified as suitable by your supervisor before the project starts.

You should not be involved in generating new data sets via surveys, experiments or any other activity involving humans or animals. Nor should you access or use any data that contains personal identifying informationLinks to an external site.. If you do, this will be treated as academic misconduct.

 

Human Participant Categories
Our collective approval covers the use of human participants only in requirements analysis and software evaluation activities. Your project will fall into one of the following categories.

0	No use of human participants in any activity
1	Human participants used during requirements analysis
2	Human participants used during software evaluation
3	Human participants used during both requirements analysis and software evaluation
Very few projects involve requirements analysis in the software engineering sense. The most likely reason for this would be if your project has an external client and you need to speak to them or their colleagues. Your project falls into this category if you will need to speak to anyone other than your supervisor about what they want or expect your software to do. Talking to your supervisor about their project idea, or to help flesh out your own idea, does not fall under this guidance.

Most software development projects will benefit from evaluation with other users. Your project falls into this category if you will ask other people to use your software (or watch you using it) and provide feedback via any of the permitted activities. Testing your own software as it is developed, and then running the software to check for issues, does not fall under this guidance if you are the only person involved.

Please note that conducting a survey is explicitly not permitted as a primary project topic. It meets neither the module learning outcomes nor the project criteria of our programme accreditation. You can use a small questionnaire as part of your requirements analysis or evaluation, but any other kind of survey using human participants will be treated as academic misconduct.

 

Obtaining Participant Consent
If you plan to conduct requirements analysis or software evaluation with other people, you must ensure you recruit and treat them ethically. The following guidelines must be followed.

You should not expose participants to any sensitive topics or require them to reveal sensitive information. Where necessary, ask them to enter dummy (false) data into your software.
You should only use participants who can give informed consent (so that means not using children or anyone with a learning or communication impairment).
You should only carry out activities that are permitted under this guidance.
You should explain to participants that their involvement is voluntary and they can drop out at any time. If they drop out, you should delete any data collected from them.
You should make it clear that they are taking part in a University of Liverpool project that follows our policy on research ethics.
You should give a participant information sheet to everyone who takes part. This will cover the final two bullet points for you. Each person must also sign a participant consent form. Links to these documents are provided at the top of this page. You should give the completed forms to your supervisor. They will be retained until the project module has been marked and processed by the boards of examiners, then they will be destroyed.

 

Permitted Activities
You can involve participants in any of the following activities. Tick the relevant boxes on the participant consent form so they know what to expect. You can conduct these activities with individuals or with a group of people at the same time.

Interviews — Asking participants a series of questions to find out what they want your software to do (requirements), or what they think of the finished product (evaluation). The questions should be included as an appendix in your dissertation.
Discussions — Similar to interviews, but conducted in a way that allows follow-up questions, tangential conversations, and so on. You might start with a basic set of questions or things to find out, but let the discussion flow naturally.
Observations — Watching participants use an existing piece of software or carry out a manual process that your software will automate or improve, or watching them use your finished product to see how they interact with it.
Narrations — Asking participants to speak aloud as they use software or perform a manual task, to describe what they are doing and why they are doing it that way. This is the same activity as observations but with more structured talking.
Questionnaires — The participants answer a set of questions, usually via an online survey. This is more useful when there are lots of participants or you don't need to be physically in the same room as them. The questions should be included as an appendix in your dissertation.
With the permission of participants, you can make audio recordings of what they say as they take part in these activities, so you can focus on running the activity instead of making written notes. If you do this, you should transcribe the audio as soon as possible afterwards and then delete the original recordings. Note that you cannot make video recordings of participants. If you need to see what they are doing, conduct a live observation or narration.

If the activity involves using software, you can use screen recordings to capture what participants are doing, where they are clicking, and so on. You can match this with audio recordings. You should take care not to record personal or private information by mistake. For example, if you are recording a mobile phone screen, ask the participants to turn off notifications from other apps so they are not accidentally captured. Make sure there is nothing personal in the background of desktop recordings. Do not record users as they enter login credentials, unless it's part of the evaluation of a login process for your software, in which case they should be using dummy credentials.

 

Protecting Participant Data
Participant data includes anything obtained from other people as you conduct your requirements analysis or software evaluation. This might include their name, initials, or some other way to identify them before you aggregate the results, along with the data they provide, such as interview transcripts, audio recordings, screen recordings, questionnaire results, the data they input into your software as they use it, and data about how they use it (for example, log files and crash dumps). Such data should always be treated according to the following rules.

Should only be collected with the permission of the participants.
Should be stored securely on a password-protected computer or your university file store.
Should not be shared with anyone else.
Should be deleted after graduation.
When you have collected data from all participants (or sooner if possible) you can start to aggregate it. That means taking the individual answers and combining them into anonymous results (tables, statistics, comments) that can then be included in your assessments. Unless you use an online questionnaire (which automatically aggregates answers), use a separate piece of paper or file for each participant. If they ask to withdraw from the activity, or later ask you to delete their data, you can do this easily by destroying the paper or file. After you have aggregated all responses into an anonymous format, you must destroy or delete the original data so there is no possibility of anyone being identified.

When you write your dissertation you will probably want to include text and comments from participants. In all cases this should be anonymised, and you should make sure you have their permission to use what they said. You should not use any comments (or any other kind of data) that could inadvertently identify anyone.

 

Software Evaluation with Dummy Data
It's likely that you will require participants to enter data into your software so they can fully evaluate it. They should be advised to enter 'false' data to avoid any possibility of personal information being stored or processed by your software. For example, if you are creating an app to advise on diet and exercise, you would probably want users to enter their age and weight, and possibly other personal items. During development, you can perform unit tests and input validation by pretending to be other people and entering dummy data. During evaluation, participants should not need to enter real data because their focus should be to provide feedback on things like appearance, overall functionality, navigation, and so on. This can be achieved just as successfully with 'fake' data.

If your software has a 'sign up' or 'registration' function to create user accounts, you will need to test that this works, but you can do so by pretending to be other people and creating fake accounts. If you ask participants to test the registration function during evaluation, advise them to enter false details. Do not under any circumstances collect real names, addresses, photos, location data, passwords, or any other personal information that could compromise anonymity or security.

If you are building a mobile app, you might want to use Apple's TestFlight facility or Google's Play Console. These systems allow you to deploy your apps to beta testers and collect data about how the app performs, along with other metrics. To do this, you will need to obtain the Apple or Google ID of each participant, which is usually an email address. You must obtain their permission to use their ID in this way (by including it on the participant consent form), and you should only use it for this purpose. If a participant doesn't want to give you their ID, they can still take part in your evaluation if you allow them to use the app on your own device.

Project Supervision
You will be allocated a supervisor who will offer help and advice throughout the project. The role of the supervisor is to ensure you stick to the deadlines, and advise you when writing your final report and other documents that form part of the assessment.

In general, you should not treat your supervisor as a guru who can solve all your problems. Very often you might find that you have more detailed knowledge than them, especially if you are working in a new development environment. Your supervisor might not be willing or able to help with tricky coding problems, although they will be able to refer you to the right resources.

Your working relationship with your supervisor will be unique. You shouldn't worry if your experience differs to those of your friends. You'll be treated as an individual, so your meetings will vary in scope and content depending on the project topic. Remember that each supervisor has to balance their workload across many areas, so they might not always be available to meet with you urgently.

You should meet with your supervisor for a 30 minute session every two weeks, but this is only a guideline and it will vary across the period of the project. If you are progressing well and your supervisor agrees, you can meet less often.

Stages of a Project
The module learning outcomes are based on the typical stages of software development. Whilst each project is unique and will be driven by you, every project follows this general process. Even if you're not developing original software (for example, if your project has more of a research focus) these steps are still applicable.

Stage	Development Focus	Research Focus
Reading	This provides the context for your project. Identify and absorb related work, pinpoint the key ideas you will build on within the project, and acquire any new skills and techniques you will need.
Requirements	Establish the user requirements for your software by speaking to your supervisor and any other stakeholders.	Determine the overarching research question and establish an experimental hypothesis.
Design	Produce a formal design using a standard notation, including a detailed explanation of the component parts and how they fit together.	Produce a detailed plan of activity, explain how technology will be employed, and list the criteria that will determine successful completion.
Realisation	Implement the software according to the design, including technical development (coding) and organisation of the working environment (such as version control).	Carry out the research plan by applying a suitable technology (or combination of technologies) to solve a problem or prove a hypothesis.
Evaluation	Check that the software implementation works as expected (testing) and demonstrate that it meets the needs of its users (evaluation).	Analyse and discuss experimental results, ensuring the research method is robust and replicable, and show that the technology was applied correctly.
Although this table is presented as discrete and sequential steps, we recognise that modern software development no longer follows the traditional waterfall model. Your approach might be agile or involve rapid prototyping (or one of many other development methods) but these activities are still present in some form.

The project spans both semesters, so it's a good idea to keep some kind of written record of your thoughts and activity, even things that don't work or lead you down the wrong path. We recommend you use a notebook (either physical or electronic) to keep a log of your progress. This won't be seen by anyone, and it's not assessed, but it will help you to remember what you did so you can refer back to it when you produce your final dissertation.

Transferrable Skills
You will be using skills you've learned in other modules, but you will also need to develop and demonstrate new skills. Some of these will be technical, but there's a wide range of other skills that are just as important. These also form part of the module learning outcomes.

Planning — A long-term piece of work requires careful planning to ensure a successful outcome. You need to work out what needs to be done, in what order, and with what importance. You need to establish how long to spend on each part of the project, to ensure you hit the required deadlines and your own internal goals.
Risk Analysis — All projects involve an element of risk. You need to identify what could go wrong and have a plan to mitigate problems as they arise. It's not possible to foresee all risks, but you can prepare for the most likely scenarios.
Ethical Conduct — You must consider the legal, social and ethical aspects of your project, and ensure you follow our ethical guidance. This is a key part of the professional accreditation of your degree.
Communication — You should be able to communicate complex technical details to non-specialists (verbally, visually and in writing). The project assessments expect you to demonstrate an ability to communicate clearly. This is a skill that will remain useful throughout your future career.
Self-Reflection — You need to be able to look critically and objectively at your work and the progess you've made. This includes identifying the negative aspects of your project (anything that didn't go to plan) and demonstrating that you've learned something valuable from the experience.
The Chartered Institute for IT (BCS) has a Code of ConductLinks to an external site.. This sets out guidelines for the proper conduct of a software professional undertaking a project. Some of these are intended for large, multi-person teams in a commercial setting, but you should be aware of them and apply their principles where it makes sense for your project.

Marking Guidelines
Marking Process
Your work will be independently marked by your supervisor and the second marker. Your supervisor writes most of the feedback and the second marker acts in a moderation role. This ensures consistency of marking across all supervisors. Usually the two marks are close to each other and the average is taken. If the marks are further apart, the markers will discuss your work and reach an agreement. Very rarely, a third marker can be called upon to give their opinion.

The marking scheme for each assessment shows the criteria on which you will be judged. These are linked in the table above. You will also receive some written feedback and suggestions for improvement. Make sure you meet your supervisor to discuss feedback on the detailed proposal, so you can incorporate any suggestions into the implementation. Feedback on the video (and during the discussion) will help you to shape your dissertation.

Marks and feedback are released in batches, due to the number of students and staff involved in the process. This depends on both your markers having completed the work, so you might not get feedback at the same time as your friends. If you think your feedback is taking too long, please contact your supervisor or the project coordinator.

Proficiency in English
In accordance with Appendix A, 2.1--2.3 of the Code of Practice on Assessment, a good knowledge of written English use must be demonstrated. This includes, minimally, correct spelling and grammar. You should also avoid over (and inaccurate) use of technical jargon (e.g. ``exponential increase'', ``decimate'') and the substitution of exotic polysyllabic constructions where simpler alternatives are available (e.g. ``do quickly'' is better than ``realise expeditiously'', use ``use'' not ``utilize''. In general, short simple words are better than involved lengthy equivalents. Similarly avoid cliche and hackneyed expressions where possible (e.g. ``state-of-the-art'', ``hot topic'', ''user friendly''

Basic proficiency in English will be a point considered in assessing written components of the project and misuse of English may have an effect on the mark given.

Academic Misconduct
Your work should be written and presented by you, in your own words. This includes source code for software development projects. You can of course make use of code libraries, plugins, APIs and other software services, provided you correctly acknowledge and reference them. The penalties for academic misconduct are severe (see Appendix L in the Code of Practice on Assessment).

The best way to demonstrate that your work is genuinely your own is to meet with your supervisor regularly and show your progress. This way, they can see the development of your original ideas, be aware of the problems you encountered, and understand how your code is put together. It's a good idea to turn up to supervision meetings with your laptop (or screen share online) so you can show what you've been working on. When writing the dissertation, acknowledge any code libraries that were not developed by you, and understand how to properly cite and reference text that was written by somebody else.

The use of generative AI is currently permitted by the university (for code and written work) provided you properly acknowledge it. In simple terms, if you reference the work that was auto-generated, this does not count as misconduct. If you attempt to pass off generated code or text as your own, this does count as misconduct. Please see the full student briefing for more information.
