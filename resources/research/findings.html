I. What was measured

The question and the design

One question, asked three ways.

Does the way a retriever is built change how well a language model can answer questions about a company's annual report? Three retrieval paradigms index the identical corpus and are asked the identical questions through the identical answering model. Anything that differs between them is the paradigm, because nothing else is allowed to vary.

1

P1 Vector

Semantic. Dense embeddings in ChromaDB with fastembed, reranked by a local cross-encoder. Matches on meaning.

2

P2 BM25

Statistical. rank_bm25 over the same nodes, no model of any kind. Matches on terms and their rarity.

3

P3 Structural

A LlamaIndex summary tree per filing, traversed by comparing the query against node summaries. Matches on position in a hierarchy.

What is held constant

  • The corpus. All three index the same 18,297 nodes from the same 13 filings, produced by one parsing and splitting pass.
  • The questions. All three answer the same 100 questions at the same three depths, giving 900 cells.
  • The answering model. One model writes every answer, so differences in answer quality trace back to what each retriever handed it.
  • The grader. One judge scores every answer against the same rubric, with a fingerprint recorded per row so a score can be tied to the rubric in force when it was written.
  • What a pipeline is never shown. The question and its own retrieved nodes, and nothing else. No ground truth, no exemplars, no hint about where the answer lives, and no metadata pre-filter.

The apparatus

Which model did which job, and why no model ever grades its own work.

Two invariants hold across this table. The generator that proposes a question is never the critic that accepts it, and the answerer that writes an answer is never the judge that scores it. Every call runs at temperature 0, and the answerer and judge are each pinned to a single upstream host so that two calls in the same run cannot land on differently quantised copies of the same model.

III. The instrument

The judge and its gate

Answers are scored 1 to 5 against the ground-truth answer only. Citation validity is checked deterministically elsewhere and deliberately kept out of the judge's remit, so a model's guess cannot override a mechanical check.

Score 5

Fully correct

Matches the ground truth, complete and precise, in a form that answers the question asked.

Score 4

Right value, short of complete

Correct on the value but falling short on completeness, precision or framing.

Score 3

Half an answer

One half of a two-part answer, with the other half absent.

Score 2

Right area, wrong figure

Reaches the right part of the filing but reports the wrong figure, ordinal or scope.

A score of 1 is wrong, unsupported, fabricated, or a refusal to answer. Pass rate on this page means 4 or 5; fail rate means 1.

IV. Results

The headline

Semantic and statistical retrieval are indistinguishable on this benchmark. BM25 leads the vector pipeline on every point estimate, but the paired difference in judge score is -0.160 with a 95% interval of [-0.340, +0.013], which spans zero. They tie on 231 of 300 cells.

The structural pipeline trails both by roughly two judge points, with intervals nowhere near zero, and it is also the most expensive of the three to build and to query.

The average conceals a split. Inside the quadrants the two leading pipelines are not interchangeable: BM25 wins the implicit-text quadrant by a margin whose interval is clear of zero, while the vector pipeline leads on implicit tables. The pooled null result is real, and it is not the whole story.

Precision is the one metric that should not be read as a ranking across depths. Depth helps, but it does not rescue

Every pipeline improves as k rises from 2 to 5. The structural pipeline improves the most in relative terms and is still far below where the other two start.

Depth is therefore not a confound in the headline comparison. The ordering of the three pipelines is the same at every depth, so no choice of k within this sweep would have produced a different conclusion.

This is the finding to lead a discussion with. A null result at the corpus level is not evidence that the two paradigms are interchangeable; it is evidence that their strengths are differently distributed and happen to cancel over this question mix. A different mix would not have cancelled.

V. Why

Retrieval decides the answer

The whole benchmark reduces to one question: did the evidence come back? Split every cell on that and the answer outcome is nearly determined.

Mean judge score 4.51 with the evidence, 1.14 without it.

P2 BM25

Evidence retrieved

88%pass rate over 252 cells

Evidence missed

4%pass rate over 48 cells

Mean judge score 4.54 with the evidence, 1.29 without it.

P3 Structural

Evidence retrieved

77%pass rate over 62 cells

Evidence missed

7%pass rate over 238 cells

Mean judge score 4.24 with the evidence, 1.26 without it.

Retrieval, not generation, is what separates these architectures. When the evidence node is returned, every pipeline passes at a similar high rate. When it is not, every pipeline fails at a similar high rate. The answering model is the same in all six of those cells, so the difference between architectures is entirely a difference in what they hand it.

19 of the 900 cells break that rule, scoring 4 or 5 with no evidence node retrieved at all. Those were answered from the answering model's own knowledge of these companies rather than from the filing, and 16 of them belong to the structural pipeline. This is a floor on parametric leakage, and it flatters the weakest pipeline rather than the strongest.

The direction of that leakage matters for the conclusion. A pipeline that retrieves nothing useful leaves the model free to answer from memory, so leakage raises the floor under the worst performer. The measured gap between structural and the other two is therefore a lower bound on the true gap.

The structural pipeline returns the highest table share on table questions and also the highest on prose questions, which is the signature of a retriever whose selectivity is weaker rather than better: it is not discriminating more sharply, it is drifting toward tabular blocks generally.

Why the structural pipeline trails

A result this lopsided invites the suspicion that something is broken. These diagnostics say otherwise, and they locate the failure precisely.

PipelineDistinct / slotsDiversity Median positionMedian missMiss, share of filing Slots on evidence
P1 Vector 430 / 1,000 0.430 0.444 76 6.1% 28.3%
P2 BM25 424 / 1,000 0.424 0.476 38 3.0% 29.1%
P3 Structural 506 / 1,000 0.506 0.596 324 29.2% 6.9%
  • It retrieves, it just retrieves the wrong nodes. Hit rate is 20.7% against 84.0% for P2 BM25: on most questions the supporting node is never returned at all. Diversity is the highest of the three, so this is not a retriever stuck returning the same nodes regardless of the query.
  • It is not landing near the evidence and picking the wrong paragraph. Its median returned node sits at 0.596 of the way through a filing whose evidence sits at 0.354, and its median miss is 29.2% of the filing against 3.0% for P2 BM25. It is landing in the wrong part of the document, biased late.
  • Tree traversal descends by comparing the query against summaries, and a summary is exactly the artefact from which a specific figure has been compressed away. On a benchmark dominated by extraction that is fatal. It would not be on a benchmark of thematic questions, which is the use this structure was designed for.
  • Its relative best quadrant is the inferential one. Where the answer appears verbatim nowhere, summarisation loses least, and the gap narrows.

The honest framing for the write-up is that this result characterises summary-tree retrieval as configured here, on an extraction-heavy benchmark. It is a measurement of a paradigm against a task, not a verdict on the paradigm in general.

What it costs to build

Latency and tokens describe what a question costs once the index exists. They leave out the one-off cost of creating it, which is where the three paradigms diverge hardest. Two build locally from the same 18,297 nodes with no model call. The third drives an LLM over every node of every filing.

P2 BM25 0.9 s

VI. Validity

Threats to validity, and how to answer them

Every limitation an examiner could raise, what the data actually shows about it, and the argument to make. Ordered by how much work each one needs in the write-up.

material

Latency does not separate the three paradigms

What the data shows. Median per-cell latency is 3.54 s for P1 Vector, 3.71 s for P2 BM25, 3.53 s for P3 Structural, a spread of 0.18 s across all three. The means differ more, but they are pulled by a small number of very slow calls against a shared hosted provider, not by retrieval work done locally.

How to put it in the write-up. Report query latency as a null result and say why: every cell routes through the same answering model, so the shared network call dominates the measurement and local retrieval time is lost inside it. The efficiency claim rests on index build cost and on tokens per query, which are measured cleanly and do separate the paradigms.

material

Some answers were correct without the evidence being retrieved

What the data shows. 19 of the 900 cells scored 4 or 5 while retrieving no ground-truth node at all, 16 of them in the structural pipeline. Those answers came from the answering model's own knowledge of these companies, not from the filing.

How to put it in the write-up. Disclose it as a floor on parametric leakage and note the direction: it inflates the weakest pipeline's score, because a pipeline that retrieves nothing useful leaves the model free to answer from memory. The measured gap between structural and the other two is therefore a lower bound on the true gap, not an exaggeration of it.

material

One run per cell, so there is no run-to-run variance

What the data shows. Every cell was executed once at temperature 0, with each model pinned to a single upstream host so that quantisation could not vary between calls. The confidence intervals describe variation across questions.

How to put it in the write-up. State plainly that the intervals are question-level, not run-level, and that the design trades repeated sampling for breadth: 900 distinct cells rather than a smaller matrix sampled repeatedly. Temperature 0 and host pinning are what make a single run defensible; say both.

material

The judge gate measures concordance, not human agreement

What the data shows. The gate compares the Judge against a reference scoring of the same 60 outputs produced under a blinding protocol, not against an independent human annotator. It reads 52 of 60 exact (86.7%), with every disagreement confined to a single band.

How to put it in the write-up. Call it cross-scorer concordance throughout and never call it human validation. The defensible claim is that the Judge is consistent with an independent application of the same published rubric under blinding, which is what the gate actually tests. Cite the blinding protocol: pipeline identity and citation evidence were withheld from the reference scorer.

moderate

The answer keys were corrected after the pipelines were built

What the data shows. Twenty-four of the 100 questions had evidence sets that named a whole filing section rather than the supporting nodes, which made full recall arithmetically unreachable. Those were re-derived to the minimal verified evidence set before the final run.

How to put it in the write-up. Argue it on mechanism, not on trust. The retention rule is mechanical, uniform, and was applied blind to every pipeline's output, so nothing could be tuned toward a preferred result; and the repair replays from the pre-repair backup to reproduce the current database exactly. Note also that it was not common-mode: it changed the vector against BM25 ordering on precision, which is precisely why leaving it in place and disclosing it would not have been defensible.

Questions are not evenly spread across the filings

What the data shows. The most-sampled filing carries 17 questions and the least-sampled carries 1. Per-filing means are therefore built on very different sample sizes.

How to put it in the write-up. Use the per-filing table as a spread check only, never as a ranking, and say so where you present it. Every headline figure is paired within a question, so the imbalance cancels: each pipeline answers exactly the same questions over exactly the same filings.

The structural pipeline's build cost is a floor, not a total

What the data shows. Eleven of the 13 filings recorded their token spend; two did not, because a logging defect was fixed after those two builds had already run. The reported figure sums the 11 that were measured.

How to put it in the write-up. Report it as a measured floor over 11 filings and name the two that are missing. The direction is safe: the true cost is higher than the figure quoted, so the conclusion that the structural index is the most expensive to build is strengthened by the gap, not threatened by it.

A small number of questions are redundant or self-answering

What the data shows. Two pairs ask the same question of the same filing, a third pair splits one fact across two quadrants, and three questions restate most of their own answer in the question text. At most six of 100.

Disclose the count and argue common-mode: all three pipelines answer the same six, so the effect is a very slight compression of the measured gap rather than a shift in the ordering. Removing them after retrieval behaviour was known would have been the less defensible choice.

One quadrant tests a narrower skill than its definition implies

What the data shows. Six of the 25 implicit-table questions share two task shapes: counting the filing's signatories, and summing page numbers in an index.

How to put it in the write-up. Frame it as a construct-coverage limitation of that quadrant, not a validity defect in any row, and be specific about why the rows are still valid: counting signatories requires retrieving an intact tabular block, which is exactly the structure-versus-semantics contrast being measured.

The corpus carries parsing artefacts from the source filings

What the data shows. 1,089 nodes contain an unescaped HTML entity, seven contain a raw tag, and four carry a corrupted digit run where a financial figure should be. Exactly one benchmark question cites a corrupted node.

How to put it in the write-up. Argue it as common-mode and, for the one affected question, as a feature: all three pipelines index the identical corpus, and on that row the answer key records what the node actually says, so the row rewards faithful extraction over a plausible figure recalled from training. Messy conversion is a property of the problem domain rather than an accident of this build.

The retrieval depths are 2, 3 and 5 rather than 3, 5 and 10

What the data shows. The sweep was narrowed on instruction after the design was written. Three depths are retained, so the 900-cell matrix is unchanged.

Record it as a scope decision with its consequence stated: the deepest setting is now 5, which sits at or above the largest corrected evidence set, so full recall stays reachable at the top of the sweep. Note that the token budget written against a maximum depth of 10 becomes a conservative over-estimate rather than a wrong one.

What would weaken these findings

The findings above would not survive all of these equally. Stated plainly, so the claims can be scoped to match.

  • A different question mix would move the vector against BM25 result. This is the most fragile finding on the page. The two are separated inside quadrants and cancel over the pooled set, so the mix of question types is doing the work. State the mix whenever the null result is quoted.
  • A thematic or summarisation benchmark would move the structural result. Summary trees compress detail away, which is fatal for extraction and may be helpful for synthesis. Nothing here tests synthesis over a whole filing.
  • A different answering model could change the leakage floor. A smaller model with less memorised knowledge of these companies would leak less and would widen the measured gap; a larger one would narrow it.
  • Nothing here would move by adding runs. The gaps between structural and the other two span roughly two judge points with intervals hundreds of times wider than any plausible run-to-run noise at temperature 0.
  • Nothing here would move by adding depth within this sweep. The ordering is identical at every one of the three depths.

Reproducibility ledger

What can be reproduced, and from what.

  • Every figure on this page is computed at build time. No number is typed into the template. Re-running the generator against the database reproduces the page.
  • The statistics are seeded. The bootstrap uses 10,000 resamples from a fixed seed, so intervals reproduce exactly rather than approximately.
  • Every judged row carries a rubric fingerprint, a digest of the grading model together with the exact system prompt, which embeds the rubric and that quadrant's calibration exemplars. Recomputing the fingerprint from the current rubric reproduces the stored value in all four quadrants, which is what rules out silent rubric drift mid-run.
  • Both answer-key corrections replay from the repository against the pre-repair backup and reproduce the current database row for row.
  • The corpus is frozen. 13 filings and 18,297 nodes; the manifest in the repository is the authoritative list.
  • Every cell is accounted for. 900 primary cells and 60 gate rows, all scored, with no nulls in any metric column.
